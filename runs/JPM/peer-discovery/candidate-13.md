## Our thesis

- JPMorgan Chase is the world's largest diversified bank with $4.4 trillion in assets, acting as the central nervous system for global finance through its leading investment bank and massive retail reach. It generated record annual revenue of $279.75 billion in 2025, and recently reported a blockbuster Q2 2026 with $21.16 billion in net income.
- Owning the stock is a bet on the widening "fortress" advantage of a bank that is outspending its peers to automate and scale. Its massive $15 billion annual technology budget creates a moat in operational efficiency and fraud detection that smaller rivals cannot replicate, allowing it to compound earnings even as interest rate cycles shift.
- The next phase of growth rests on pragmatic AI integration and a push to double its Swiss private banking business by 2030, which diversifies income into fee-rich wealth management. This reduces reliance on volatile lending income and targets the high-growth ultra-high-net-worth segment globally.
- JPMorgan trades at approximately 14.6 times its trailing earnings, a fair valuation for a dominant compounder that is currently delivering a 17.8% return on equity. While Jamie Dimon has cautioned that the economy is "close to as good as it gets," the firm's raised guidance for $105.5 billion in net interest income suggests growth persists.
- The primary risk is a severe economic downturn that spikes loan losses or new regulatory rules that force the bank to hold more idle capital. We maintain a positive view because JPMorgan's scale and diverse revenue streams from trading and fees provide a safety net that protects shareholders during market stress.

## Business Overview

What does it do?

JPMorgan Chase is a mature financial services business that earns money by collecting interest on loans and charging fees for financial services. The bank takes in deposits from consumers and businesses, paying a small amount of interest, and lends that money out at higher rates for mortgages, credit cards, and corporate loans. It also acts as a middleman in global markets, earning fees for helping companies go public (IPOs), advising on mergers, and executing stock and bond trades for institutional investors. This dual model of steady lending income and high-margin service fees makes it the dominant force in American and global finance.

Where does revenue come from?

Revenue is split between the interest earned on its $4.4 trillion asset base and fees from its massive service platform. The Consumer & Community Banking segment provides nearly half of its income through mortgages and credit cards, while the Commercial & Investment Bank drives roughly one-third via trading and advisory fees. Asset & Wealth Management provides a stable, growing fee base by managing trillions for wealthy individuals. Geographically, while it is a global power, the vast majority of its profit is still anchored in the North American market.

Who are its customers?

JPMorgan Chase serves 82 million consumers and 6 million small businesses in the U.S., alongside nearly all the world's largest corporations and governments. Its retail arm, Chase, provides banking services to nearly half of all U.S. households, while its private bank manages assets for a significant portion of the global billionaire class. The Commercial & Investment Bank serves over 80% of the Fortune 500, providing the complex cross-border financing and risk management these firms require. In the most recent quarter, Equities trading revenue alone surged 86% to $6.03 billion, reflecting intense demand from its institutional hedge fund and asset manager clients.

What gives it staying power?

JPMorgan's staying power comes from its "fortress balance sheet" and its $15 billion annual technology spend. Customers choose JPMorgan because they know it can survive any crisis, and its massive investment in AI and digital apps creates high switching costs for users who rely on its seamless ecosystem.

Where is it headed?

The bank is making a massive strategic bet on integrating AI into every corner of its operations to replace manual human tasks. Management is using its scale to open 160 new branches in 2026 while simultaneously expanding its EMEA wealth management footprint. If successful, this "clicks and bricks" strategy will lower its cost to serve customers while capturing more high-fee advisory revenue.

## Financial Performance

JPMorgan is currently operating at peak efficiency, with Q2 2026 revenue of $82.46 billion significantly beating estimates. While this headline number was lifted by a $4.6 billion one-time gain from a Visa share exchange, the organic growth was equally strong. Even excluding one-time items, adjusted earnings grew 13% year-over-year, proving the bank can grow despite a plateauing interest rate environment.

The balance sheet is the strongest in the industry, with $4.4 trillion in assets supporting a 17.8% return on equity. As a global bank, its debt is essentially its product, but its Tier 1 capital ratio—a key measure of bank safety—remains well above regulatory requirements. This position allowed management to raise its full-year net interest income guidance to a record $105.5 billion.

JPMorgan remains the gold standard of financial strength, delivering record profits that are increasingly driven by technology and trading dominance.

What's Working Well

The Commercial and Investment Bank is seeing a massive surge in market activity, with equity trading revenue jumping 86% to $6.03 billion. This growth is driven by a rebound in IPOs and heavy trading volumes from institutional clients. Years of consistent investment in trading technology are now paying off as the firm captures a larger share of global market flows.

What to Watch

Higher regulatory capital requirements remain the biggest threat, as they could force the bank to hold more cash instead of returning it to shareholders. Management has expressed frustration with shifting rules that might restrict buybacks. Investors should monitor whether these "Basel III" endgame rules significantly dampen the firm's ability to maintain its 17% return on equity.

Industry Stage Mature Industry

The global banking industry is valued at over $150 trillion and grows roughly in line with GDP, yet it is currently undergoing a massive digital shift. Pricing power is generally weak in commodity lending, but it is structural in complex investment banking and wealth management where expertise and scale are paramount. JPMorgan Chase is the undisputed leader of this industry, acting as the "too big to fail" incumbent that paradoxically grows faster than its smaller peers. Its massive scale allows it to absorb regulatory costs that are crushing regional banks, creating a permanent consolidation tailwind.

The Competition

The competitive landscape is rationally structured among the "Big Four" U.S. banks, but it is becoming a winner-take-all market for technology leaders. While entry barriers for new banks are high due to regulation, the true battle is for deposits and trading fees. JPMorgan's massive size allows it to outspend every other bank on the planet, creating a digital-first trap that makes it hard for competitors to lure away customers.

Bank of America and Citigroup remain the primary scale threats, using their massive branch networks to compete for consumer deposits. However, Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley are the more dangerous rivals in high-margin advisory and wealth management services. The most significant threat comes from Morgan Stanley's dominance in wealth management, which directly challenges JPM's goal of doubling its private bank.

JPMorgan is currently gaining market share in nearly every segment, particularly in trading and investment banking where its revenue surged 27% in the most recent quarter.

How is the fair value calculated?

We use a Price-to-Book Value (P/B) approach anchored to Return-on-Equity (ROE), the standard framework for valuing large, diversified banks. It fits JPMorgan because the bank's net worth (book value) grows steadily each quarter, and its superior 17.8% ROE—how much profit it makes on every dollar of that net worth—justifies a significant premium over its peers.

Multiplying the current book value of $139.78 per share by a 2.7x multiple gives us a fair value of $380. This 2.7x multiple sits at the top of the large-bank peer range (Bank of America 2.2x, Wells Fargo 1.4x, Citigroup 0.7x), a premium justified by JPMorgan's significantly higher profit margins and its unmatched $15B annual technology budget. We use the most recent Q2 2026 equity base of $374.6 billion divided by 2.68 billion shares to establish the $139.78 starting point.

Cross-check

A Forward P/E (price-to-earnings) cross-check results in a fair value of $372, within 2% of our primary answer and confirming the result. Using the consensus FY2027 earnings estimate of $24.81 and applying a 15x multiple—which is the upper end of its historical 10-14x range—reflects the market's willingness to pay more for "quality" in a shifting interest rate environment. This close alignment between the book-value math and the earnings-power math gives us high confidence that $380 represents the true intrinsic value of the franchise today.

What are the assumptions?

We're assuming JPMorgan sustains a 17% Return on Equity (ROE) — a measure of how much profit the bank generates with shareholders' money. The firm just reported a 17.8% ROE in Q2 2026 and has averaged roughly 17.4% over the last three years, suggesting its competitive moat in scale and technology is protecting these high-tier returns.

We're assuming Net Interest Income (NII) — the core profit from loans minus interest paid to depositors — stays near $105.5 billion. Management recently raised guidance to this record level, and while falling interest rates usually hurt banks, JPMorgan’s massive deposit base and diverse loan book allow it to defend these margins better than smaller rivals.

We're assuming the bank continues to reinvest $15 billion annually into technology and AI. This massive spend, which exceeds the total annual profits of most regional banks, creates a permanent cost advantage by automating fraud detection and back-office operations that competitors simply cannot afford to match.

What's the biggest risk?

The biggest risk is a "hard landing" recession that triggers a wave of defaults across credit cards and small business loans. This economic shock would likely force the price-to-book multiple down from 2.7x to 1.8x, knocking roughly $125 off the per-share fair value. Watch the "Provision for Credit Losses" in the next two earnings reports for any sustained move above $4.5B.

What could change the price?

Bear case ($290): Net interest income (the profit from lending) drops below $95B as the Fed cuts rates faster than management can adjust its balance sheet; or Quarterly credit loss provisions (money set aside for bad loans) spike above $5B due to rising defaults in the Apple Card and subprime auto portfolios.

Bull case ($440): Investment banking fees sustain 25%+ growth as the dealmaking cycle accelerates into 2027, offsetting any pressure from lower interest rates; or The $15B annual technology investment drives the efficiency ratio (operating costs as a percentage of revenue) below 50% through AI-driven automation.
